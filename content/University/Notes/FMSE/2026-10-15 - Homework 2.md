## 0. What this lecture is about

> [!abstract] The story in 4 sentences
> 1. **DPLL** (lecture 1) guesses values, propagates (BCP), and on a conflict undoes **only the last** decision. It does not remember *why* the conflict happened, so in another branch it can run into the same conflict again.
> 2. **CDCL** (*Conflict-Driven Clause Learning*) asks at every conflict: **"which assignments are really to blame?"**. It reads the answer from the **implication graph**, a record of what was implied by what.
> 3. The guilty assignments form a "forbidden combination". Its negation is a new clause (the **learned clause**), which we add to the formula so that this combination never occurs again.
> 4. Then the solver **backjumps** to the earliest level at which the learned clause already implies something, skipping decisions unrelated to the conflict.
>
> The rest of the lecture (minimization, LBD, VSIDS, restarts) are **heuristics** that speed this mechanism up.

> [!tip] Analogy
> You get lost in a maze. DPLL goes back to the last fork and tries the other path. CDCL writes on a sheet of paper: "corridor X together with corridor Y always leads into a dead end", goes straight back to the place where it chose X, and from then on never combines X with Y.

## 1. Recap: BCP and DPLL (slides 1–3)

> [!note] Theory: a clause under a partial assignment
> Under a partial assignment every clause is in one of 4 states:
> - **satisfied** – at least one literal is true,
> - **falsified (conflict)** – all literals are false,
> - **unit** – all literals are false except one, which is still unassigned. That one **must** become true,
> - **unresolved** – at least two literals are unassigned.

> [!note] Theory: BCP (Boolean Constraint Propagation)
> 1. Find a unit clause (ℓ).
> 2. Remove all clauses containing ℓ (they are satisfied).
> 3. Delete ¬ℓ from the remaining ones (it is false).
> 4. Repeat until a **fixpoint** or until the **empty clause** (conflict).
>
> BCP **guesses nothing** – everything it forces is a logical consequence.

> [!note] Theory: DPLL
> ```
> DPLL(F):
>   F := BCP(F)
>   if F = ∅  → SAT          (no clauses = everything satisfied)
>   if ∅ ∈ F  → UNSAT        (empty clause = conflict)
>   choose a literal l
>   if DPLL(F ∧ l) = SAT → SAT
>   return DPLL(F ∧ ¬l)
> ```
> DPLL is **sound and complete**, but it backtracks **chronologically** (by one decision only) and **remembers nothing** from conflicts.

> [!quote]- From the notes (A. Tang): DPLL with pure literals (Algorithm 1)
> M = set of literals (partial assignment), N = set of clauses.
> ```
> function DPLL(M, N)
>   if all clauses in N are true in M then          ▷ N is satisfied in M
>     return true
>   else if some clause in N is false in M then     ▷ N is not satisfied in M
>     return false
>   else if there is a unit clause φ = ℓ in N then  ▷ Unit propagation
>     return DPLL(M ∪ {ℓ}, N)
>   else if there is a pure literal ℓ in N then     ▷ Handle pure literals
>     return DPLL(M ∪ {ℓ}, N)
>   else
>     Choose a literal ℓ from N that is undefined in M   ▷ decision literal
>     if DPLL(M ∪ {ℓ}, N) then return true
>     else return DPLL(M ∪ {¬ℓ}, N)
> ```
> - A **pure literal** is one whose complement does not appear anywhere. Since a pure literal can be made true without bad consequences, any clause containing a pure literal can be deleted.
> - A **unit clause** is a clause containing only one literal. The literal must be set as true, so its complement can be deleted from all clauses (unit propagation / BCP).

## 2. Trail, decision levels, reason (slides 5–6)

> [!note] Theory: concepts
> - **Trail** – the list of assignments in the order in which they were made. The order matters (section 6)!
> - **Decision level** – every decision opens a new level. Everything BCP derives from it has the **same** level.
> - **Level 0** – facts independent of guessing (unit clauses from the input, *top-level units*). They are never undone.
> - **Notation** `x = 1 @ 3` (or shorter `x@3`) – x is true, assigned at level 3. `x = 0 @ 3` is ¬x@3.
> - **Reason / antecedent** – the clause that forced the literal via BCP. Decisions have **no** reason (in the implementation a `null` pointer, slide 6).

> [!tip] How to read a clause as a rule
> A clause with one positive literal is an implication:
> `(¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s ∨ t)` ≡ `d ∧ g ∧ s → t`.
> When d, g, s are true, the clause is unit and forces t. This clause is then the **reason** for t.

> [!example] Worked example
> F = (¬a ∨ b) ∧ (¬b ∨ c) ∧ (¬d ∨ ¬c ∨ e). Decisions: a, then d.
>
> | Trail | Level | Reason |
> |---|---|---|
> | a | 1 | decision |
> | b | 1 | (¬a ∨ b) |
> | c | 1 | (¬b ∨ c) |
> | d | 2 | decision |
> | e | 2 | (¬d ∨ ¬c ∨ e) |
>
> Note: e has level 2, even though it also depends on c from level 1. **The level of a literal = the level at which it was assigned** (i.e. the highest level among its causes).

## 3. Implication graph (slides 5–9)

> [!note] Theory: definition (slide 5)
> The implication graph is a directed acyclic graph (DAG):
> - **Nodes** – literals from the trail, labelled with their level (`x=1@d`).
> - **Edges** – if ℓ₂ has reason C, then from every **other** literal of C (more precisely: from the assignment that falsified it) there is an edge to ℓ₂, labelled with C.
> - **κ (conflict node)** – has no outgoing edges. Its incoming edges come from the assignments that falsified the **conflicting clause**.
>
> Properties:
> - decisions are **sources** (no incoming edges),
> - the graph is **acyclic**, because a literal is implied only by literals assigned **earlier**.

> [!quote]- Definition from slide 5 (verbatim)
> An implication graph is a labelled directed acyclic graph G(V, E), where
> - V represents the literals of the current partial assignment (trail).
> - E represents the reasons of assignments:
>   $E = \{(\ell_1, \ell_2) \mid \ell_1, \ell_2 \in V,\ \ell_1 \neq \ell_2,\ \neg\ell_1 \in \mathit{Reason}(\ell_2)\}$
>
> Labels:
> - Nodes are labelled with their corresponding literal and decision level ($x = v\,@d$ where $v \in \{0, 1\}$, but we will often use a simpler notation)
> - Edges are labelled with the reason clause used for propagation
>
> Special node: κ (conflict node)
> - No outgoing edges
> - Incoming edges: $\{(\ell, \kappa) \mid \neg\ell \in C,\ C \in F\}$ labelled with C for some falsified (conflicting) clause C
>
> From the notes: *Each propagated literal is a node, with edges from the literals that forced it. The conflict (a falsified clause) is a special sink node in the graph. The graph is guaranteed to be acyclic; decision literals are always source nodes.*

> [!tip] What is the graph for?
> The graph is the "investigation record". Going backwards from κ along the edges, you see **all causes** of the conflict. Whatever cannot be reached from κ going backwards has nothing to do with the conflict.

> [!example] Worked example (slide 6)
> F = C1 ∧ C2 ∧ C3 = (¬a ∨ ¬b ∨ c) ∧ (¬b ∨ ¬c) ∧ (¬a ∨ b). Decision: a = 1.
> - C3: a true → **b=1@1**, edge a → b (C3).
> - C2: b true → **c=0@1**, edge b → ¬c (C2).
> - C1 = (¬a ∨ ¬b ∨ c): everything false → conflict. Edges a → κ, b → κ, ¬c → κ (C1).
> ```
> a=1@1 ──C3──▶ b=1@1 ──C2──▶ c=0@1
>   │             │              │
>   └─────C1──────┴──────C1──────┴──▶ κ
> ```
>

## 4. Clause learning and cuts (slides 10–11)

> [!note] Theory: where the learned clause comes from (slide 10)
> - The literals that contributed to the conflict form a partial assignment, e.g. c ∧ f.
> - This assignment **leads to a conflict**, so it cannot occur in any model.
> - Its negation ¬(c ∧ f) = **(¬c ∨ ¬f)** is a clause implied by F.
> - After adding it, the solver will **never again** build the same assignment.

> [!note] Theory: cut (slide 11)
> A **cut** splits the nodes of the graph into two sides:
> - the **reason side** – contains all decisions,
> - the **conflict side** – contains κ.
>
> Let L be the nodes on the reason side whose outgoing edge is **cut through**. Then
> **conflict clause = ∨ ¬ℓ for ℓ ∈ L**.
>
> Why does this work? Together, the literals in L force (via BCP) everything on the conflict side, including κ. So they cannot all be true.

> [!quote] Slide 11 (verbatim)
> **Cut**: Separates conflict node from all roots (decisions).
> Let L be the set of nodes whose outgoing edge is cut through. Then, **Conflict Clause**: $\bigvee_{\ell \in L} \neg\ell$.
>
> ![[FMSE-W2-slajd11.png|600]]

> [!important] Key property (slide 12)
> If the cut edges leave ℓ₁, …, ℓₖ, then
> **F ⊨ (¬ℓ₁ ∨ … ∨ ¬ℓₖ)**, so **F ≡ F ∧ (¬ℓ₁ ∨ … ∨ ¬ℓₖ)**.
> Adding the clause **does not change the set of models**. There are many cuts, and each gives a correct clause. The only question is **which one to pick** (section 5).

> [!example] Worked example (graph from slide 6)
> Graph: a → b → ¬c, all three → κ.
> - Cut right before κ: L = {a, b, ¬c} → clause (¬a ∨ ¬b ∨ c). This is just the conflicting clause C1.
> - Cut right after a: L = {a} → clause **(¬a)**.
>
> Both are implied by F. The second is much stronger: it says directly "a must be false".

## 5. UIP, first UIP, asserting clause (slide 12)

> [!note] Theory: definitions
> - **UIP (Unique Implication Point)** – a node at the **current** level through which **every** path from the last decision to κ passes. In graph terms: a *dominator* of the decision w.r.t. κ.
> - The decision itself is always a UIP (trivially).
> - **First UIP (1UIP)** – the UIP **closest to κ**.
> - **Asserting clause** – a conflict clause with **exactly one** literal from the current decision level.

> [!quote]- Slide 12 (verbatim)
> **Observation**: If edges intersected by a cut are ℓ₁ → k₁, …, ℓₖ → kₖ, then F ⊨ (¬ℓ₁ ∨ · · · ∨ ¬ℓₖ)
> → We can learn it while maintaining logical equivalence F ≡ F ∧ (¬ℓ₁ ∨ · · · ∨ ¬ℓₖ)
>
> But how to find cuts in practice? And which conflict clause to learn at the end?
> - **Asserting Clause**: A conflict clause with a single literal from the current decision level.
> - **Unique Implication Point (UIP)**: A node in the implication graph such that all paths from the last decision to the conflict node goes through it.
>     - a dominator for the current decision w.r.t. κ
>     - intuition: a single reason at the current level that causes the conflict
> - **First UIP**: The UIP that is closest to κ.
>
> From the notes: *Using 1UIP ensures the learned clause has exactly one literal from the current decision level. This is efficient and empirically effective.*

> [!tip] Intuition
> A UIP is the "bottleneck" of the current level: a single cause that alone (together with facts from lower levels) is enough for the conflict.
> A clause with exactly one literal from the current level has a great property: after undoing the current level, that literal is the only unassigned one, so the clause **immediately becomes unit** and forces something. Hence the name "asserting".

> [!question] Why the first UIP and not the decision?
> - A clause from the decisions depends on **what was guessed**. The first UIP clause depends on **what was implied**, and the same thing can be implied by other decisions. So it is **more general** and useful more often.
> - It is **asserting**, so it can be used for backjumping.
> - It is usually short. Empirically it is the best choice.

> [!example] Worked example (slide 6)
> Graph: a → b → ¬c → κ, plus a → κ, b → κ. Paths from a to κ: a→κ, a→b→κ, a→b→¬c→κ. Only a is common. **UIP = a = first UIP.**

## 6. Computing the first UIP clause by resolution (slides 13–23)

> [!note] Reminder: resolution (lecture 1)
> ```
> (C ∨ v)    (D ∨ ¬v)
> ───────────────────
>       (C ∨ D)
> ```
> The variable v is the **pivot**. Literals occurring in both C and D appear once in the result.
> **Self-subsuming resolution**: if D ⊆ C, the result is simply C – i.e. you drop v from (C ∨ v).

> [!important] Theory: algorithm (slide 13)
> 1. C := the conflicting clause.
> 2. **While** C contains more than one literal from the current level:
>    - ℓ := the one among them that was assigned **last** on the trail,
>    - C := the resolvent of C and **reason(ℓ)** (pivot = the variable of ℓ).
> 3. Return C – this is the first UIP clause. Its only literal from the current level is the first UIP.
>
> Remarks:
> - Every intermediate C corresponds to a **cut** in the graph, moved one node to the left (**resolvents = cuts**, slide 18).
> - We resolve only on literals from the **current** level. Literals from lower levels stay in the clause unchanged.
> - Why "last assigned"? Because then we never jump over a UIP – we stop at the first one (closest to κ).
> - The result does not depend on the order of propagation – the first UIP clause is unique.

> [!quote]- Slide 13 (verbatim)
> Compute by resolution the clauses that are associated with cuts. Simple algorithm:
> 1. Let the conflicting clause C₀ be the first conflict clause
> 2. Pick the literal ℓ ∈ C₀ that was last assigned
> 3. While there is more than one literal in Cᵢ that is assigned at the current level:
>     1. Let Cᵢ₊₁ be the resolvent of Cᵢ and the reason clause of ℓ
>     2. Let ℓ be the literal ℓ′ ∈ Cᵢ₊₁ that was last assigned
>
> Remarks:
> - Every conflict clause Cᵢ corresponds to a cut in the implication graph
> - The last Cᵢ is the first UIP-clause → it is an asserting clause

> [!example] Worked example (formula B, current level 3)
> Order at level 3: d, e, f, g, h.
>
> | Step | Clause C | Lits at level 3 | Last assigned | Reason |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 0 | (¬c ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬h) | g, h | h | C6 = (¬f ∨ h) |
> | 1 | (¬c ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬f) | g, f | g | C5 = (¬f ∨ g) |
> | 2 | **(¬c ∨ ¬f)** | only f | stop | |
>
> Compare with task 4.1: steps 0, 1, 2 are exactly the cuts (a), (b), (c). **Learned clause: (¬c ∨ ¬f), first UIP = f.**

## 7. Backjumping (slides 3–4, 24–25)

> [!note] Theory: backtracking vs backjumping
> - **Chronological backtracking** (DPLL): undo the **last** decision and try the opposite value.
> - **Non-chronological backjumping** (CDCL): undo **many** levels at once, skipping decisions that did not contribute to the conflict ("bad decisions", slide 4).
> - To know which decisions contributed, we need conflict analysis – and the learned clause tells us exactly that.

> [!important] Theory: the backjump rule (slide 24)
> 1. Learn the asserting first UIP clause.
> 2. Jump to the **second highest decision level** occurring in that clause. If the clause has one literal → **level 0**.
> 3. At that level all literals of the clause except the first UIP are false, so BCP **immediately forces** the negation of the first UIP.
> 4. Propagate further and continue the search.
>
> **Conflict at level 0 ⟹ UNSAT** (there is nothing left to undo).

> [!quote]- Slides 24–25 (verbatim)
> - Assume a non decision UIP node is found
> - Learn and use the corresponding asserting conflict clause to guide backjumping
>
> → Backjump to the second highest decision level in the asserting conflict clause
> - At that point (the negation of) the literal of the UIP node is implied by unit propagation
> - Propagate all other new assignments and continue search
>
> ![[FMSE-W2-slajd25.png|600]]

> [!question] Why "second highest" and not lower?
> It is the **lowest** level at which the clause is unit. Any lower and it would have at least two unassigned literals and force nothing. Any higher and we would keep unnecessary decisions.

> [!example] Worked example (formula B)
> Learned: (¬c ∨ ¬f). Levels: c → 1, f → 3. Second highest = **1**.
> - We undo levels 3 **and 2**. Decision x disappears, even though it was not the "last" one – this is the non-chronological jump. DPLL would undo only d.
> - Level 1: c is true → (¬c ∨ ¬f) is unit → **¬f @1**.
> - BCP continues: C4 (¬b ∨ ¬e ∨ f) with b=1, f=0 → **¬e @1**. C3 (¬d ∨ e) → **¬d @1**.
>
> Without any new decision the solver knows that d must be false.

## 8. The CDCL loop (slides 26–28)

> [!note] Theory: basic loop (slide 26)
> ```
> loop:
>   conflict = propagate()               // BCP
>   if conflict:
>       if level == 0: return UNSAT
>       analyze(conflict)                // 1UIP + learn + backjump
>       continue
>   if all variables assigned: return SAT
>   lit = decide()                       // heuristic, e.g. VSIDS
>   assign(lit, reason = null)           // new level
> ```

> [!note] Theory: a modern solver (slide 27, CaDiCaL)
> The same loop, but with extra steps executed from time to time:
>
> | Function | What it does | Section |
> |---|---|---|
> | `propagate`, `analyze` | BCP, conflict analysis | 6–7 |
> | `decide` | variable selection | 11 |
> | `restart` | backtrack to level 0 | 12 |
> | `rephase` | reset the preferred values (phases) of variables | 11 |
> | `reduce` | delete useless learned clauses | 10 |
> | `probe`, `subsume`, `elim` | inprocessing: simplifying the formula during search | lecture 1 |
>
> Features of modern CDCL (slide 28): learning + backjumping, clause minimization, clause database reduction, decision heuristics, restarts, watched literals, pre- and inprocessing.

> [!quote]- Slide 26: basic CDCL (code)
> ```c
> static int cdcl (void) {
>   for (;;) {
>     clause * conflict = propagate ();
>
>     if (conflict) {
>       if (!analyze (conflict))      // Analyze & Learn & Backjump
>         return unsatisfiable;
>       else
>         continue;
>     }
>
>     if (trail.size () == (size_t) variables)
>       return satisfiable;
>
>     int lit = decide ();
>     assign (lit, decision_reason);
>   }
> }
> ```

> [!quote]- Slide 27: CaDiCaL loop (code)
> ```cpp
> int Internal::cdcl_loop_with_inprocessing () {
>
>   int res = 0;
>
>   while (!res) {
>          if (unsat) res = 20;
>     else if (unsat_constraint) res = 20;
>     else if (!propagate ()) analyze ();      // propagate and analyze
>     else if (iterating) iterate ();          // report learned unit
>     else if (satisfied ()) res = 10;         // found model
>     else if (search_limits_hit ()) break;    // decision or conflict limit
>     else if (terminated_asynchronously ())    // externally terminated
>       break;
>     else if (restarting ()) restart ();      // restart by backtracking
>     else if (rephasing ()) rephase ();       // reset variable phases
>     else if (reducing ()) reduce ();         // collect useless clauses
>     else if (probing ()) probe ();           // failed literal probing
>     else if (subsuming ()) subsume ();       // subsumption algorithm
>     else if (eliminating ()) elim ();        // variable elimination
>     else if (compacting ()) compact ();      // collect variables
>     else if (conditioning ()) condition ();  // globally blocked clauses
>     else res = decide ();                    // next decision
>   }
>
>   return res;
> }
> ```
> 10 = SAT, 20 = UNSAT (the exit code convention of SAT solvers).

> [!note]- From the notes (A. Tang): CDCL as a transition system
> A state is `M ∥ N`: M = trail (list of annotated literals), N = set of clauses. $\ell^d$ = decision literal, $\ell^{C}$ = literal forced by clause C.
> - **Propagate**: if C is false in M and ℓ is undefined in M:
>   $M \parallel N \cup \{C \lor \ell\} \Rightarrow M\,\ell^{C \lor \ell} \parallel N \cup \{C \lor \ell\}$
> - **Backjump**: if some clause of N is false in $M_0\,\ell_0^d\,M_1$ and there is a "backjump clause" $C \lor \ell_1$ such that $N \models C \lor \ell_1$, C is false in $M_0$, and $\ell_1$ is undefined in $M_0$:
>   $M_0\,\ell_0^d\,M_1 \parallel N \Rightarrow M_0\,\ell_1^{C \lor \ell_1} \parallel N$
> - **Learn**: if $N \models C$: $M \parallel N \Rightarrow M \parallel N \cup \{C\}$
> - **Forget**: if $N \models C$: $M \parallel N \cup \{C\} \Rightarrow M \parallel N$
> - **Restart**: $M \parallel N \Rightarrow \varepsilon \parallel N$ (usually after a certain number of learned clauses or after deriving a unit)
>
> **Example (SAT):** N = (¬P1 ∨ ¬P2) ∧ (¬P1 ∨ P3) ∧ (¬P3 ∨ ¬P4) ∧ (P2 ∨ P4 ∨ P5)
> 1. ε ∥ N – nothing to propagate, decision P1: $P_1^d \parallel N$
> 2. (¬P1 ∨ ¬P2) → $\neg P_2$
> 3. (¬P1 ∨ P3) → $P_3$
> 4. (¬P3 ∨ ¬P4) → $\neg P_4$
> 5. (P2 ∨ P4 ∨ P5) → $P_5$
>
> Result: $P_1^d\ \neg P_2^{\neg P_1 \lor \neg P_2}\ P_3^{\neg P_1 \lor P_3}\ \neg P_4^{\neg P_3 \lor \neg P_4}\ P_5^{P_2 \lor P_4 \lor P_5} \parallel N$ – N is satisfied.

> [!note]- From the notes (A. Tang): two watched literals, pre- and inprocessing
> **Two watched literals** – an implementation trick for fast BCP:
> - instead of scanning all literals of a clause, the solver "watches" **two** unassigned literals in every clause,
> - for every variable P it keeps a list of clauses in which P is watched, and a list for ¬P,
> - when a variable is assigned, it checks only the clauses in which its (false) literal was watched, and moves the watch to another literal if possible. If that is not possible, the clause is unit or falsified.
>
> **Preprocessing / inprocessing** (removing tautologies and duplicate literals, and in addition):
> - **Subsumption**: if C ⊆ D, then $N \cup \{C\} \cup \{D\} \Rightarrow N \cup \{C\}$
> - **Purity deletion**: delete all clauses containing a literal whose negation does not occur in the clause set.
>   Example: (P1 ∨ ¬P3 ∨ ¬P4) ∧ (¬P2 ∨ P3) ∧ (P1 ∨ ¬P2) ∧ (¬P1 ∨ P2 ∨ P3) ∧ (¬P1 ∨ P2). ¬P4 is pure → delete the first clause. Now P3 is pure → what remains is (P1 ∨ ¬P2) ∧ (¬P1 ∨ P2).
> - **Merging replacement resolution**: if C ⊆ D, then $N \cup \{C \lor \ell\} \cup \{D \lor \neg\ell\} \Rightarrow N \cup \{C \lor \ell\} \cup \{D\}$
> - **Bounded variable elimination**: eliminating a variable by resolving all pairs (C ∨ ℓ), (D ∨ ¬ℓ), but only when the number of new clauses does not grow beyond a limit.

## 9. Learned clause minimization (slides 29–37)

> [!note] Theory: why?
> A shorter clause = **stronger** knowledge (forbids more assignments), faster BCP, less memory. The first UIP clause often contains **redundant** literals, because they are implied by other literals of the same clause.

> [!note] Theory: local minimization (slide 32)
> 1. Mark all variables of the first UIP clause.
> 2. Remove a literal if **all** literals of its reason (antecedent) are marked (or are from level 0).
>
> Why is this correct? The removal is a **self-subsuming resolution** step: we resolve with the reason, and the rest of the reason is already in the clause, so the result = the clause without that literal. The graph is acyclic, so the result is unique (confluence).

> [!note] Theory: recursive minimization (slide 36, MiniSAT)
> Like local, but you may go **deeper** into the graph:
> - for a candidate literal, search its causes backwards,
> - if **every** path ends at a marked literal (or at level 0) → remove the candidate,
> - if you reach a **decision outside the clause** (or a new level that is not in the clause) → do not remove (optimization: terminate early).
>
> You never remove a decision (decisions have no reason). The first UIP always stays.

> [!example] Worked example (slides 29–35)
> The first UIP clause from task 6.3: (¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s ∨ ¬h ∨ ¬i). Marked: d, g, s, h, i.
>
> **Locally:**
> - i: reason (¬h ∨ i) → cause h, marked → **remove ¬i**.
> - h: reason (¬e ∨ ¬g ∨ h) → e unmarked → stays.
> - d, g, s: their reasons contain unmarked a/c, f, l/r → they stay.
>
> Result: (¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s ∨ ¬h).
>
> ![[FMSE-W2-slajd30.png|600]]
>
> **Recursively** – we try h: causes e, g. g is marked. e: reason (¬b ∨ ¬d ∨ e) → d marked, b from level 0. All paths end safely → **remove ¬h**.
> As resolution (slide 34):
>
> | Step | Clause | Resolve with | Pivot | Result |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | (¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s ∨ ¬h) | reason of h: (¬e ∨ ¬g ∨ h) | h | (¬e ∨ ¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s) |
> | 2 | (¬e ∨ ¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s) | reason of e: (¬b ∨ ¬d ∨ e) | e | (¬b ∨ ¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s) |
> | 3 | (¬b ∨ ¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s) | level-0 unit: (b) | b | **(¬d ∨ ¬g ∨ ¬s)** |
>
> Out of 5 literals, 3 remain.
>
> ![[FMSE-W2-slajd34.png|600]]
> ![[FMSE-W2-slajd35.png|600]]

> [!quote]- Slides 32 and 36 (verbatim)
> **Local Minimization Algorithm.** Two step algorithm:
> 1. mark all variables in 1st UIP clause
> 2. remove literals with all antecedent literals also marked
>
> Correctness: removal of literals in step 2 are self subsuming resolution steps; implication graph is acyclic.
> Confluence: produces a unique result.
>
> **Recursive Minimization Algorithm [MiniSAT 1.13].** Four step algorithm:
> 1. mark all variables in 1st UIP clause
> 2. for each candidate literal: search implication graph
> 3. start at antecedents of candidate literals
> 4. if search always terminates at marked literals remove candidate
>
> Correctness and Confluence as in local version!
> Optimization: terminate early with failure if new decision level is “pulled in”

> [!info]- Slide 37: does minimization pay off?
> Experiments on 100 instances of the SAT'08 Race. *recur* = recursive, *local* = local, *none* = no minimization. Recursive minimization solves the most instances and uses clearly less memory (it removes about 1/3 of the literals).
>
> ![[FMSE-W2-slajd37.png|600]]

## 10. Clause database reduction and LBD (slide 38)

> [!note] Theory: why delete learned clauses?
> - Some become useless (e.g. satisfied at level 0).
> - Some are not relevant to the current part of the search.
> - Every clause costs memory and slows down BCP.
>
> So from time to time (`reduce`, geometric, Luby or arithmetic schedule) we delete "unimportant" learned clauses.

> [!note] Theory: how to choose what to keep?
> - **size** – delete long ones,
> - **LRU** – delete those not used for a long time (like a cache),
> - **activity** – a score bumped on use (like VSIDS, BerkMin),
> - **LBD (glue)** – the number of **distinct decision levels** among the literals of the clause (Audemard and Simon 2009). Smaller = better. Clauses with **LBD ≤ 2** are never deleted.

> [!quote]- From the notes (A. Tang): learned clause deletion
> The solver may, from time to time, delete some learned clauses.
> - Deletion is done at restart, or after reaching a threshold regarding the number of learned clauses.
> - Clauses involved in unit propagation cannot be deleted (they are currently the reason of some literal on the trail).
> - A solver may adopt a combination of deletion strategies — delete long clauses with higher probability.

> [!tip] LBD intuition
> Literals from the same level are related to each other (they follow from one decision). LBD tells you how many **independent decision blocks** the clause glues together. A clause with LBD 2 connects only 2 blocks – it is very "glue-like" and often used in propagation.

> [!example] Worked example
> (¬c ∨ ¬f) from formula B: c@1, f@3 → levels {1, 3} → **LBD 2** → glue, kept forever.

## 11. Decision heuristics and VSIDS (slides 39–40)

> [!note] Theory: goals of the heuristic
> Two questions: **which variable** to pick and **which value** (phase) to give it. The heuristic should be fast and lead to "easier" subproblems (shorter clauses, fewer variables, independent parts).

> [!note] Theory: kinds of heuristics
> - **Static** – one order fixed before the start. Fast, computed once.
> - **Dynamic** – count literal occurrences in clauses that are not yet satisfied. Expensive (you have to traverse the clauses) → **impractical in CDCL**.
> - **Second-order dynamic** – e.g. **VSIDS**. They do not look at the state of the formula, only at the conflict history. Cheap and effective.

> [!important] Theory: VSIDS (Variable State Independent Decaying Sum)
> 1. **Start**: the score of a variable = the number of its occurrences in the formula.
> 2. **Decision**: pick the unassigned variable with the highest score.
> 3. **Conflict (bump)**: +1 for the variables of the learned clause (or of the clauses used in the analysis).
> 4. **Decay**: every N conflicts (e.g. 256) divide all scores, e.g. by 2, and re-sort.
>
> The name: *variable state independent* – does not depend on the current assignment; *decaying sum* – old merits fade. Effect: the solver focuses on the variables of **recent** conflicts.

> [!example] Worked example (slide 40)
> Decay "divides by 2, rounding down": 3 → 1, 7 → 3, 5 → 2, 4 → 2, 2 → 1.
> Earlier on the slide: the learned clause ¬x4 ∨ x5 ∨ ¬x8 bumps x4: 2→3, x5: 3→4, x8: 6→7.
>
> ![[FMSE-W2-slajd40.png|600]]

> [!quote]- From the notes (A. Tang): VSIDS
> The idea is to associate a positive score with every propositional variable. At the start, k is the number of occurrences of p in N. The variable order is the descending ordering of the score.
> - Every time a learned clause is computed after a conflict, the propositional variables in the learned clause obtain a bonus b, so the new score is k + b.
> - The scores are levelled periodically, where the score k becomes k/l for some l.
> - The variable order is recomputed after each restart.
>
> There is no universal formula for the parameters b and l. These are determined by tuning and benchmarking.

## 12. Restarts (slides 41–42)

> [!note] Theory: why restart?
> - **SAT**: the solver can get stuck in an unsatisfiable part of the search space because of bad early decisions.
> - **UNSAT**: focusing on one part may miss a short proof.
>
> **Restart** = undo everything back to level 0. **Keep the learned clauses** (and usually the VSIDS scores) – the knowledge stays, only the search order changes.

> [!note] Theory: when to restart?
> - **Restart limit** – restart after a given number of conflicts.
> - To avoid running into the same dead end: randomness (variable or phase) and/or keeping learned clauses.
> - **For completeness the limit must grow**: inner/outer, **Luby** (1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1, 2, 4, 8, …), Reluctant Doubling (Knuth).
> - **Glucose**: exponential moving average (EMA) of LBD over a short and a long window. If the recent LBDs are worse than the long-term average → restart (the solver is learning poor clauses, so it is in a bad place).
> - Alternating between "stabilizing" mode (rare restarts) and "non-stabilizing" mode (frequent restarts).

> [!example]- Slide 42: inner/outer restart intervals
> 378 restarts in 104 408 conflicts. X axis: restart number, Y axis: conflict limit until the next restart. You can see "saw teeth": the inner limit grows, then drops, and the peak of each tooth (the outer limit) is higher and higher. This way the limit grows without bound and the solver stays complete.
>
> ![[FMSE-W2-slajd42.png|600]]

> [!quote]- From the notes (A. Tang): restart
> SAT solving is a search algorithm, and it is possible to get trapped in a local search space. The solution to this is to restart CDCL completely with a different variable ordering.
> - Heuristics may be preserved or reset.
> - Learned clauses are typically preserved to avoid losing all accumulated knowledge.

> [!warning] Common pitfalls
> - "Last assigned" is determined by **the order on the trail**, not alphabetically.
> - You resolve only on literals from the **current** level.
> - The backjump goes to the **second highest level in the learned clause**, not to "current − 1".
> - Unit clause → jump to **level 0**.
> - A literal occurring in both resolved clauses appears **once** in the result.
> - Literals that are false at level 0 can be dropped from the learned clause.
> - The level of a literal = the level at which it was **assigned**, not the lowest level among its causes.
