## 0. What this lecture is about

> [!abstract] The story in 5 sentences
> 1. A SAT solver is an engine. The lecture shows **how to build a tool on top of it**: encode the problem, ask the question, and decode the answer.
> 2. The running example is **bounded model checking (BMC)** of a 2-bit counter. We ask: "can the counter reach the value 2 in exactly k steps?".
> 3. The simplest way is to encode everything from scratch for every k and run the solver on a DIMACS file (**direct approach**). Faster is to use **one solver instance** (incremental SAT). We then only add new clauses, and pass temporary conditions as **assumptions** through the **IPASIR** interface. **IPASIR-UP** goes furthest: an external module interferes with the search already during `solve()`.
> 4. **SMT** extends SAT with theories (equality, functions, arithmetic, arrays). Instead of only Boolean variables we have formulas like x + y < 0.
> 5. The predominant approach is **lazy SMT / CDCL(T)**: the SAT solver solves the Boolean skeleton, and the theory solver checks whether the model makes sense in the theory, and if not, adds a blocking clause.

> [!tip] Analogy
> A SAT solver is a calculator.
> - **Direct approach**: every time you type in the whole problem from scratch.
> - **Incremental SAT**: a calculator with memory. You only add the new numbers, and you check "what if" without clearing.
> - **IPASIR-UP**: someone stands next to you and, during the calculation, gives hints or says "that cannot be".
> - **SMT**: a calculator that does not know geometry itself, but can ask a geometer whether the result makes sense.

## 1. SAT-based tools: overview (slides 1–3)

> [!note] Theory: the SAT solver as a black box (slide 1)
> - **Input**: a formula F in CNF.
> - **Output**: SAT + an interpretation I such that I(F) = 1, or UNSAT + a refutation (proof of unsatisfiability).
> - Main approach: **CDCL** (lecture 2). Scales to millions of variables and clauses.

> [!note] Theory: use of SAT solvers (slide 2)
> System and its requirements → **Encode** (question M ⊨ φ?) → formula → **SAT solver** → solution → **Decode** → e.g. a bug in the system.
> A model of the formula usually means a **counterexample** (e.g. a path to the bug). UNSAT means there is no bug (within the encoded bound).

> [!important] Four structures of SAT-based tools (slide 3)
>
> | No. | Name | Incremental? | Integrated? | How it talks to the solver |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | Direct Approach | no | no | DIMACS file, separate process |
> | 2 | Embedded Direct Approach | no | yes | solver API, but a new problem every time |
> | 3 | Incremental Integrated | yes | yes | one instance, IPASIR, assumptions (e.g. CEGAR) |
> | 4 | Interactive Integrated | yes | yes | IPASIR-UP, interference during `solve()` |

> [!tip] How to remember
> "Incremental" = **between** `solve()` calls the solver remembers what it already knows.
> "Interactive" = you can influence the solver **during** a single `solve()`.

## 2. Running example: 2-bit counter and BMC (slides 4–9)

> [!note] Theory: from a program to logic (slides 4–5)
> Program: the counter `i` starts at 0, in every step `i = (i + 1) % 4`, and the assertion requires `i != 2`.
> **Bit-blasting**: we write the number 0–3 with two bits.
> - a is the least significant bit, b is the most significant bit. Value = 2b + a.
> - Step: `next_a = NOT a`, `next_b = a XOR b`.
> - The value 2 is b = 1, a = 0, so the assertion is ¬(b ∧ ¬a).

> [!note] Theory: three ingredients of BMC (slide 6)
>
> | Symbol | Meaning | For the counter |
> |---|---|---|
> | I(s) | initial state | ¬b ∧ ¬a |
> | T(s, s′) | transition relation | (b′ ↔ (a ⊕ b)) ∧ (a′ ↔ ¬a) |
> | B(s) | "bad state" (property violated) | b ∧ ¬a |
>
> s = (b, a) is the current state, s′ = (b′, a′) is the state after one step. In step k we write indices: a₀, b₀, a₁, b₁, …

> [!important] Encoding BMC as SAT (slides 7–8)
> An error trace of length k exists ⟺ the formula
> **I(s₀) ∧ T(s₀, s₁) ∧ … ∧ T(sₖ₋₁, sₖ) ∧ B(sₖ)**
> is satisfiable.
> - SAT → stop, the property is violated. **The model = a counterexample** (concrete states s₀ … sₖ).
> - UNSAT → there is no violation in **exactly k** steps. Increase k.

> [!question] Does BMC prove that there is never a bug?
> No. BMC only checks lengths up to the current k. UNSAT for k = 0, 1, 2, … is not a proof for all k. A full proof requires other techniques (Block IV, model checking).

![[FMSE-W3-slajd07.png|600]]

> [!example] Worked example: the transition relation as a table (slide 6)
>
> | Counter | b | a | b′ | a′ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 0 → 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
> | 1 → 2 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 |
> | 2 → 3 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
> | 3 → 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
>
> Checking the row 1 → 2: a′ = ¬a = ¬1 = 0, b′ = a ⊕ b = 1 ⊕ 0 = 1. The state (b′, a′) = (1, 0) is the value 2. ✓

![[FMSE-W3-slajd06.png|600]]

> [!note] Theory: T in CNF (needed for the tasks)
> Every ↔ is turned into clauses (lecture 1). For one step (a, b) → (a′, b′):
>
> | No. | Clause | As a rule | Origin |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | T1 | (¬b′ ∨ a ∨ b) | b′ → (a ∨ b) | b′ → a ⊕ b |
> | T2 | (¬b′ ∨ ¬a ∨ ¬b) | b′ → ¬(a ∧ b) | b′ → a ⊕ b |
> | T3 | (b′ ∨ ¬a ∨ b) | a ∧ ¬b → b′ | a ⊕ b → b′ |
> | T4 | (b′ ∨ a ∨ ¬b) | ¬a ∧ b → b′ | a ⊕ b → b′ |
> | T5 | (a′ ∨ a) | ¬a → a′ | a′ ↔ ¬a |
> | T6 | (¬a′ ∨ ¬a) | a → ¬a′ | a′ ↔ ¬a |

## 3. Direct approach and DIMACS (slides 10–13)

> [!note] Theory: direct approach (slide 10)
> For each bound k:
> 1. Encode the **complete** BMC problem into a single SAT problem in DIMACS format.
> 2. Call a SAT solver on the produced file.
> 3. Parse & evaluate the output of the solver to map it back to a counterexample.
>
> Every k is a separate file and a separate, independent call. The solver remembers nothing.

![[FMSE-W3-slajd10.png|600]]

> [!important] DIMACS format (slide 11)
>
> | Line type | Form | Meaning |
> |---|---|---|
> | comment | `c …` | ignored |
> | header | `p cnf m n` | m = **maximum of variable indexes**, n = **number of clauses** |
> | clause | e.g. `-1 5 3 4 0` | literals as signed integers, minus = negation, **0 terminates the clause** |

> [!example] Worked example (slide 11)
> Clauses:
>
> | No. | Clause | DIMACS line |
> |---|---|---|
> | D1 | (x₁ ∨ ¬x₅ ∨ x₄) | `1 -5 4 0` |
> | D2 | (¬x₁ ∨ x₅ ∨ x₃ ∨ x₄) | `-1 5 3 4 0` |
> | D3 | (¬x₃ ∨ ¬x₄) | `-3 -4 0` |
>
> The maximum index is 5 (x₂ does not occur, but the maximum still counts), there are 3 clauses. The file:
>
> ```
> c DIMACS example
> p cnf 5 3
> 1 -5 4 0
> -1 5 3 4 0
> -3 -4 0
> ```

> [!note] Theory: embedded direct approach (slides 12–13)
> - The SAT solver is used as an embedded engine in a tool chain. Instead of files we use its **interface (API)**.
> - Pro: "Often the simplest solution is the best one".
> - Cons of both direct approaches (slide 13):
>   - the complete encoding can be **extremely large** (or impossible),
>   - not everything is relevant to find a refutation,
>   - not everything is best solved as SAT (→ SMT).

> [!quote]- Slide 13 (verbatim)
> Often the simplest solution is the best one.
> But . . .
> - Complete encoding can be extremely large (or impossible)
> - Not everything is relevant to find a refutation
> - Not everything is best solved as SAT — See later SMT problems

## 4. Incremental SAT and assumptions (slides 14–17)

> [!note] Theory: incremental SAT problems (slide 14)
> A sequence of formulas in which each one **extends** the previous one with new clauses:
>
> | Call | Formula | New clauses |
> |---|---|---|
> | 0 | F₀ = Δ₀ | Δ₀ = C₁ ∧ … ∧ Cₙ |
> | 1 | F₁ = Δ₀ ∧ Δ₁ | Δ₁ = C′₁ ∧ … ∧ C′ₙ′ |
> | 2 | F₂ = Δ₀ ∧ Δ₁ ∧ Δ₂ | Δ₂ = C″₁ ∧ … |
>
> We ask in turn: is Fᵢ = Δ₀ ∧ … ∧ Δᵢ satisfiable? The same solver instance answers all the questions.

![[FMSE-W3-slajd14.png|600]]

> [!important] Assumptions (slides 14–15)
> - **Assumptions** are **temporal unit clauses**, considered only during the **next** `solve()` call.
> - Clauses added with `add` stay permanently. Assumptions vanish once the call has finished.
> - When the problem is UNSAT **due to the assumptions**, the solver returns an **unsat core**: a subset of the assumptions that is enough for the contradiction.

> [!important] How are assumptions handled (slide 17, [Eén, Sörensson SAT'03])
> - Assumptions a₁, …, aₙ are treated as **forced decisions**: the first n decisions satisfy the assumptions in turn.
> - If a conflict would backtrack (undo) such a forced decision → **UNSAT** (under these assumptions).
> - The **unsat core** is a subset of the assumptions that makes the formula UNSAT. It is read from the **learned clause of the very last conflict analysis**.

![[FMSE-W3-slajd17.png|600]]

> [!quote]- Slide 17 (verbatim)
> How are assumptions handled? [EenSorensson-SAT03]
> - Consider them as forced decisions
> - a1, a2, . . . an assumptions → First n decisions are satisfying each assumptions
> - If a conflict would backtrack (i.e., undo) such forced decision, return UNSAT
>
> How are unsatisfiable cores built?
> - Subset of assumptions that makes the formula UNSAT
> - Learned clause of the very last conflict analysis

> [!question] Why is UNSAT under assumptions not UNSAT of the formula?
> Assumptions are a "what if?" question. The answer UNSAT means: "under these assumptions it is impossible". The formula itself may be satisfiable (as on slide 15). That is exactly why assumptions are useful: you can ask many questions without spoiling the formula.

> [!note] Theory: temporary clauses via assumptions (slide 16)
> IPASIR has no "delete clause" function. Workaround:
> 1. Extend every temporary clause with an **activation variable** a: (a ∨ ℓ₁ ∨ … ∨ ℓₙ).
> 2. Solve under the assumption ¬a. Then the clause must be satisfied by one of the ℓᵢ, i.e. it is **activated**.
> 3. Once the clauses are not needed any more, permanently add the unit clause (a). BCP at the root level trivially satisfies them, and the next garbage collection deletes them.
>
> Warning from the slide: "Use with care: Can ruin solver performance significantly in certain cases."

![[FMSE-W3-slajd16.png|600]]

> [!example] Worked example (slide 15)
>
> | No. | Clause | As a rule |
> |---|---|---|
> | F1 | (a ∨ b) | ¬a → b |
> | F2 | (a ∨ ¬c) | c → a |
> | F3 | (¬b ∨ d) | b → d |
>
> | Query | Assumptions | Result |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | none | **SAT**, e.g. a, b, ¬c, d |
> | 2 | {¬a, ¬b, c} | **UNSAT**: ¬a forces b (F1), but we assumed ¬b. Core: {¬a, ¬b} |
> | 3 | {¬c} | **SAT**: the previous assumptions vanished |

- [ ] **4.4 (slide 16)** You want to temporarily add two clauses and then "delete" them. Write down what you add, under which assumptions you solve, and how you deactivate them.

> [!abstract] Given
> Permanent formula: F1 (a ∨ b), F2 (a ∨ ¬c), F3 (¬b ∨ d).

> [!success]- Answer

- [ ] **4.5 (slide 17)** Why does a conflict that would have to undo a forced decision mean UNSAT, and not "try something else"?

> [!success]- Answer
> A forced decision is an assumption, and an assumption by definition must be true in this call. Undoing it means changing the question. Since the learned clause demands negating one of the assumptions, the formula is contradictory under all the assumptions together.

## 5. Incremental BMC and IPASIR (slides 18–22)

> [!important] Incremental SAT solver in BMC (slide 18)
> One solver instance, and for consecutive k:
>
> | k | Add permanently (`add`) | Assume | Result |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 0 | I(s₀) | B(s₀) | UNSAT, core: B(s₀) |
> | 1 | T(s₀, s₁) | B(s₁) | UNSAT, core: B(s₁) |
> | 2 | T(s₁, s₂) | B(s₂) | SAT, model {¬b₀, ¬a₀, ¬b₁, a₁, b₂, ¬a₂} |
>
> - I and T stay forever, because they are true for every k.
> - B(sₖ) **must not** stay permanently, because for k + 1 the bad state has to be in sₖ₊₁, not in sₖ. That is why it is an assumption.
> - B = b ∧ ¬a is a conjunction of literals, so we simply assume bₖ and ¬aₖ.

> [!note] Theory: IPASIR (slides 19–20)
> **IPASIR** = "Re-entrant Incremental Satisfiability Application Program Interface". A standardized interface for incremental SAT, used also at the annual competition [Balyo, Biere, Iser, Sinz, JAI'16]. Supports interactions **between** `solve` calls.
>
> | Function | Role |
> |---|---|
> | `ipasir_init` / `ipasir_release` | create / release the solver |
> | `ipasir_add(lit_or_zero)` | add a literal to the current clause, 0 terminates the clause (as in DIMACS) |
> | `ipasir_assume(lit)` | an assumption for the next `solve` |
> | `ipasir_solve` | solve |
> | `ipasir_val(lit)` | value of the literal in the model (after SAT) |
> | `ipasir_failed(lit)` | whether the assumption is in the core (after UNSAT) |
> | `ipasir_set_terminate` | callback for termination |
> | `ipasir_set_learn` | callback with learned clauses (up to max_length) |
>
> Result codes as in CaDiCaL (lecture 2, slide 27): **10 = SAT, 20 = UNSAT**.

![[FMSE-W3-slajd20.png|600]]

> [!quote]- Slide 20 (verbatim, ipasir.h)
> ```c
> IPASIR_API const char * ipasir_signature ();
> IPASIR_API void * ipasir_init ();
> IPASIR_API void ipasir_release (void * solver);
> IPASIR_API void ipasir_add (void * solver, int32_t lit_or_zero);
> IPASIR_API void ipasir_assume (void * solver, int32_t lit);
> IPASIR_API int ipasir_solve (void * solver);
> IPASIR_API int32_t ipasir_val (void * solver, int32_t lit);
> IPASIR_API int ipasir_failed (void * solver, int32_t lit);
> IPASIR_API void ipasir_set_terminate (void * solver, void * data,
>     int (* terminate)(void * data));
> IPASIR_API void ipasir_set_learn (void * solver, void * data, int
>     max_length, void (* learn)(void * data, int32_t * clause));
> ```

> [!note] Theory: IPASIR solver states (slide 24)
> The diagram is on slide 24 ("IPASIR-UP – Interface of Interactive SAT"), but it describes the plain IPASIR functions. IPASIR-UP extends them (section 6). The descriptions of `val` and `failed` in the function table above are the standard IPASIR semantics. Slide 20 only gives the signatures.
>
> | State | How you get there | What is allowed |
> |---|---|---|
> | UNKNOWN | `init`, every `add` / `assume` (from any state except SOLVING) | `add`, `assume`, `solve` |
> | SOLVING | `solve` | wait (or interrupt → UNKNOWN, "interrupted") |
> | SAT | `solve` finished with a model | `val`, `add`, `assume`, `solve` |
> | UNSAT | `solve` finished with a contradiction | `failed`, `add`, `assume`, `solve` |

![[FMSE-W3-slajd24.png|600]]

> [!important] Incremental SAT solvers: benefits (slide 21)
> - Reuse the exact same solver instance:
>   - smaller initial encoding,
>   - can reuse previous reasoning steps instead of repeating them: keep **learned clauses**, keep gathered information (**phases, scores**), keep applied **formula simplifications**.
> - Assumptions provide some influence over the search.
> - The IPASIR interface makes SAT solvers **interchangeable**.
> - Used in: BMC, planning, MaxSAT, lazy SMT.

## 6. IPASIR-UP: interactive SAT (slides 23–25)

> [!note] Theory: incremental SAT solving is not always enough (slide 23)
> - Interaction is possible **only once the solving is finished**.
> - Worst case: complete encoding of domain knowledge up front, which is not always practical.
>
> Solution: we encode the problem **partially**, and during the search an external module **inspects and influences** it. Use cases: combinatorial problems, SMT, MaxSAT.

![[FMSE-W3-slajd23.png|600]]

> [!important] IPASIR-UP: what an external propagator can do (slide 25, [Fazekas et al. SAT'23])
> **Inspect search**: the solver notifies **all changes to the trail** (assignments, backtracking).
> **Influence search**:
>
> | No. | Capability | Callback |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | add propagation steps | `cb_propagate` |
> | 2 | dictate decisions & phases | `cb_decide`, `phase(lit)` |
> | 3 | add new clauses (anytime!) | `cb_add_external` |
> | 4 | overrule found solutions | `cb_check_found_model` |
> | 5 | explain relevant propagations (give the reason) | `cb_add_reason` |

![[FMSE-W3-slajd25.png|600]]

> [!question] What is `cb_add_reason` for?
> CDCL needs the **reason** of every propagated literal to build the implication graph and learn a clause (lecture 2). If a literal came from outside (`cb_propagate`), the solver must ask: "which clause does this follow from?". It only needs to ask about the propagations that take part in conflict analysis ("relevant propagations"), hence the lazy explanation.

## 7. What is SMT (slides 26–30)

> [!note] Theory: from SAT to SMT (slides 26–27)
> - **SAT**: is there an assignment of true and false to the Boolean variables such that the formula evaluates to true?
> - **SMT** (Satisfiability Modulo Theories): the formula contains **equality, functions, predicates, arithmetic…**. Is there an assignment of **values** to the variables (and an interpretation of the symbols) such that the formula evaluates to true?
> - **Input** of an SMT solver: a formula involving a theory T (**not in conjunctive normal form**). **Output**: SAT + an interpretation (F is T-satisfiable) or UNSAT + a refutation.
> - Formally (slide 28): for a first-order formula F and background theories T we look for an interpretation I such that **I ⊨_T F**.

> [!important] Example theories of SMT (slides 29–30)
>
> | Theory | Example from the slide | What is known about the symbols |
> |---|---|---|
> | Equality logic | x = y ∧ y = z ∧ u ≠ x ∧ z = u | variables of arbitrary domain, only = and ≠ |
> | Equality logic with uninterpreted functions (EUF) | x = y ∧ y = z ∧ u ≠ x ∧ z = G(x, u) ∧ G(y, z) = G(x, u) | functions are unrestricted (apart from being functions) |
> | (Linear) arithmetic | (x + y ≤ 1 ∧ 2x + y = 1) ∨ 3x + 2y ≥ 3 | variables are numbers, standard interpretation of +, ≤ |
> | Arithmetic in general | (x · y ≤ 1 ∧ 2x + y = 1) ∨ y² ≥ 3 | also multiplication of variables |
> | Bit vectors | – | most often reduces essentially to SAT (bit-blasting) |
> | Arrays | – | types of array, index, element |
> | Quantifiers | ∀x ∃y. x + y = 0 | quantifiers |

![[FMSE-W3-slajd29.png|600]]

> [!example] Worked example (slide 27)
> (x + y < 0) → (x < 5 ∧ p). The solver returns {x = 6, y = 0, p = ⊤}.
> Check: x + y = 6, so x + y < 0 is false, and an implication with a false antecedent is true. ✓
> Note: x < 5 is false, and yet the model is fine.

## 8. Axioms of EUF and arrays (slides 31–33)

> [!important] Example axioms – theory of EUF (slide 31)
>
> | Name | Axiom |
> |---|---|
> | Reflexivity | t = t |
> | Symmetry | s = t ⇒ t = s |
> | Transitivity | (s = t ∧ t = u) ⇒ s = u |
> | Congruence | s₁ = t₁ ∧ … ∧ sₙ = tₙ ⇒ f(s₁, …, sₙ) = f(t₁, …, tₙ) |
>
> **Congruence** is the core of EUF: equal arguments give equal results. Nothing else is known about the function.

![[FMSE-W3-slajd31.png|600]]

> [!tip] Why EUF in verification (slide 31)
> EUF is used to **abstract away details**: complicated operations (multiplication, addition) are replaced by uninterpreted functions. If the formula is already contradictory after abstraction, you do not need to understand the arithmetic.

> [!important] Example axioms – theory of arrays (slide 32)
>
> | Name | Axiom |
> |---|---|
> | read-over-write 1 | ∀a ∀i ∀v. read(write(a, i, v), i) = v |
> | read-over-write 2 | ∀a ∀i ∀j ∀v. i ≠ j ⇒ read(write(a, i, v), j) = read(a, j) |
> | extensionality | ∀a ∀b. (∀i. read(a, i) = read(b, i)) ⇔ a = b |
>
> In words: you read from the position you just wrote to → you get the written value. You read from a different position → you get what was there before. Two arrays are equal ⟺ they have the same elements.

![[FMSE-W3-slajd32.png|600]]

> [!note] Theory: SMT-LIB (slide 33)
> SMT-LIB (smt-lib.org) is an initiative standardizing theories, logics and their combinations.
> - Not all of them are **decidable**.
> - Not all of them are supported by all solvers.

> [!example] Worked example (slide 31)
> Original: s = t ∧ (s · (REG[i] + 2)) = u ∧ (t · (REG[i] + 2)) ≠ u
> Abstraction: s = t ∧ mul(s, add(REG(i), 2)) = u ∧ mul(t, add(REG(i), 2)) ≠ u
>
> | Step | Conclusion | Axiom |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | let X = add(REG(i), 2). We have s = t and X = X | assumption, reflexivity |
> | 2 | mul(s, X) = mul(t, X) | congruence |
> | 3 | mul(s, X) = u, so mul(t, X) = u | symmetry, transitivity |
> | 4 | contradiction with mul(t, X) ≠ u | – |
>
> **UNSAT** without any knowledge about multiplication.

> a = b and b = c → a = c (transitivity) → f(a) = f(c) (congruence) → contradicts f(a) ≠ f(c). **UNSAT.**

- [ ] **8.3** Is f(a) = f(b) ∧ a ≠ b satisfiable in EUF? And g(g(x)) = x ∧ g(x) ≠ x?

> [!abstract] Given
> EUF axioms: reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, congruence. The functions are uninterpreted: you can define them however you like.

> [!success]- Answer
> - f(a) = f(b) ∧ a ≠ b: **SAT**. Congruence only works in one direction (equal arguments ⇒ equal results, not the other way round). E.g. a = 0, b = 1, f constant = 5.
> - g(g(x)) = x ∧ g(x) ≠ x: **SAT**. E.g. x = 0, g(0) = 1, g(1) = 0. Then g(g(0)) = g(1) = 0 ✓, g(0) = 1 ≠ 0 ✓.

## 9. Solving SMT: eager vs lazy (slides 34–36)

> [!important] Main solving approaches (slide 34)
>
> | Approach | Idea | Examples |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Eager** | translate the problem into an **equisatisfiable** pure SAT problem | fragments of arithmetic, bit-blasting bit vectors, EUF (Ackermann) |
> | **Lazy: CDCL(T)** | solve the **propositional abstraction** as SAT, while the theory solver refines the problem and guides the search | z3, cvc5, MathSAT, Yices2 |
>
> Lazy is the **predominant approach**, because it benefits from specialized theory-specific inference methods.

![[FMSE-W3-slajd34.png|600]]

> [!note] Theory: division of work in lazy SMT (slide 35)
>
> | SAT solver | Theory solver |
> |---|---|
> | solves the **Boolean skeleton** of the problem | decides T-satisfiability of a **conjunction of theory literals** |
> | its models are checked by the theory solvers | recognizes conflicting subsets or propagates literals |

![[FMSE-W3-slajd35.png|600]]

> [!important] Theories and theory solvers (slide 36, [Barrett, Tinelli 2018])
> - **Theory T**: a set of axioms in first-order logic.
> - **T-satisfiability of F**: F ∧ T is satisfiable (F is T-consistent).
> - **T-model M**: M ⊨ F and M is T-consistent.
> - **T-solver**: receives a set of theory literals ℓ₁, …, ℓₙ and answers T-satisfiable / T-unsatisfiable. It should be:
>
> | Property | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | Incremental | literals arrive one at a time (along with the SAT trail) |
> | Backtrackable | can undo literals when the SAT solver backjumps |
> | Conflict Set Generation | on a contradiction, gives a **small** conflicting subset of literals |
> | Theory Propagation | derives theory literals that must hold by itself |
> | Explanation Generation | for every propagation it can give an explaining clause (reason) |

![[FMSE-W3-slajd36.png|600]]

> [!tip] Connection to IPASIR-UP
> These properties match section 6 one-to-one: trail notifications = incremental and backtrackable, `cb_propagate` = theory propagation, `cb_add_reason` = explanation generation, `cb_add_external` / `cb_check_found_model` = adding a clause from the conflict set.

- [ ] **9.1 (slide 34)** Eager or lazy? Justify.

> [!abstract] Given
>
> | Description | Content |
> |---|---|
> | (a) | 32-bit addition is replaced by an adder circuit, i.e. clauses over bits, and given to a SAT solver. |
> | (b) | The SAT solver proposes an assignment of the atoms x ≤ 3, x ≥ 5, and the arithmetic module answers "contradictory, add (¬p ∨ ¬q)". |
> | (c) | Every occurrence of f(x), f(y) is replaced by fresh variables and we add x = y ⇒ f_x = f_y, then everything is translated to SAT up front. |

> [!success]- Answer
> - (a) **eager**: bit-blasting, the whole translation up front.

- [ ] **9.2 (slide 36)** Why must a T-solver be backtrackable, and why should the conflict set be small?

> [!success]- Answer
> - **Backtrackable**: the SAT solver backjumps (lecture 2). Theory literals from undone levels no longer hold, so the T-solver has to "forget" them without starting from scratch.
> - **Small conflict set**: the learned clause is built from the conflict set. A shorter clause forbids more assignments at once (like minimization in lecture 2). The whole model as a conflict set would block only that one model.

## 10. Lazy SMT step by step (slides 37–38)

> [!important] Algorithm (slides 37–38)
> 1. Construct the **Boolean skeleton** φᴮ: replace every theory atom with a fresh Boolean variable.
> 2. Ask a SAT solver for a model I of the skeleton. UNSAT → φ is UNSAT.
> 3. Ask a theory solver whether the literals of I are **T-consistent**. A literal that is false in I means the **negated** atom (e.g. ¬b is x ≠ y + 1).
> 4. If not: take the reason (a conflicting subset), add the **Boolean skeleton of the negated reason** to φᴮ as a clause, and go back to 2.
> 5. If yes: φ is **SAT**, and the theory model gives the solution.

> [!example] Worked example (slides 37–38)
> φ := z + y ≥ 0 ∧ (x = y + 1 ⇒ z + x < 0) ∧ z = f(x)
>
> | Variable | Atom |
> |---|---|
> | a | z + y ≥ 0 |
> | b | x = y + 1 |
> | c | z + x < 0 |
> | d | z = f(x) |
>
> Skeleton φᴮ:
>
> | No. | Clause |
> |---|---|
> | L1 | (a) |
> | L2 | (¬b ∨ c) |
> | L3 | (d) |
>
> | Round | SAT model | Query to the theory | Answer |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | {a, b, c, d} | z + y ≥ 0 ∧ x = y + 1 ∧ z + x < 0 ∧ z = f(x) | **not consistent**. Reason: from x = y + 1 we get z + x = z + y + 1 ≥ 1, but it must be < 0. T ⊨ ¬(a ∧ b ∧ c) |
> | – | add L4 = (¬a ∨ ¬b ∨ ¬c) | | |
> | 2 | {a, ¬b, c, d} | z + y ≥ 0 ∧ x ≠ y + 1 ∧ z + x < 0 ∧ z = f(x) | **consistent**, e.g. z = 1, y = 1, x = −2, f constantly 1 |
>
> Checking the solution: z + y = 2 ≥ 0 ✓, x = −2 ≠ y + 1 = 2 ✓, z + x = −1 < 0 ✓, f(−2) = 1 = z ✓.
> The reason in round 1 does not contain d: the atom z = f(x) plays no part in the contradiction. This is a **small conflict set**.

### Tasks

- [ ] **10.1 (slides 37–38)** Cover the example. Build the Boolean skeleton and run the first round of lazy SMT.

> [!abstract] Given
> φ := z + y ≥ 0 ∧ (x = y + 1 ⇒ z + x < 0) ∧ z = f(x)
> Atoms: a ↔ z + y ≥ 0, b ↔ x = y + 1, c ↔ z + x < 0, d ↔ z = f(x).
> Assume the SAT solver returns the first model I = {a, b, c, d}.

> [!success]- Answer
> Skeleton: we write the implication x = y + 1 ⇒ z + x < 0 as (¬b ∨ c), so φᴮ = (a) ∧ (¬b ∨ c) ∧ (d).
> Theory: substituting x = y + 1 into z + x gives z + y + 1. Since z + y ≥ 0, z + x ≥ 1, so it cannot be < 0. Conflicting subset {a, b, c}.
> We add **(¬a ∨ ¬b ∨ ¬c)**. Now every model of the skeleton has ¬b: a is forced by (a), so with b the clause (¬b ∨ c) requires c, and the new clause requires ¬c. Hence the model {a, ¬b, c, d} of round 2 (c can be anything).

- [ ] **10.3** What would happen in 10.2 if the theory solver returned the **whole** model as the reason, e.g. (¬p ∨ q ∨ ¬r ∨ ¬u ∨ ¬w) in round 1?

> [!abstract] Given
> Skeleton as in 10.2: K1 (p ∨ q), K2 (r), K3 (u), K4 (w). Model of round 1: I₁ = {p, ¬q, r, u, w}.

> [!success]- Answer
> The clause would block only I₁. The SAT solver could return e.g. {p, q, r, u, w}, which is also theory-inconsistent, and another round would be needed. For large formulas the number of rounds grows exponentially. A small conflict set (K5 without q and u) forbids **at once** all models with p, r, w.

- [ ] **10.4 (slide 36, theory propagation)** On formula S from 10.2: before the SAT solver guesses anything, it has r, u, w at level 0. What could the theory solver **propagate** right away, and with what explanation?

> [!abstract] Given
> Atoms: p ↔ x ≥ 3, q ↔ y ≥ 3, r ↔ x + y ≤ 2, u ↔ x ≥ 0, w ↔ y ≥ 0.
> On the trail (level 0): r, u, w.

> [!success]- Answer
> - From r and w: x + y ≤ 2, y ≥ 0 ⇒ x ≤ 2, so **¬p** (x < 3). Explanation (reason): **(¬r ∨ ¬w ∨ ¬p)**.
> - From r and u: analogously **¬q**, explanation **(¬r ∨ ¬u ∨ ¬q)**.
>
> Then K1 = (p ∨ q) is immediately falsified and the solver finishes with UNSAT **without any wrong model**. This is the same knowledge as K5 and K6 from task 10.2, but obtained earlier. That is the advantage of theory propagation (and of `cb_propagate` + `cb_add_reason` in IPASIR-UP).

> [!success]- Answer
> - **P1** `add` = permanent, assumption = only for the next `solve`. If in BMC you added B(s₀) with `add`, for k = 1 the formula would require a violation in s₀ **and** s₁. It would always be UNSAT (B(s₀) contradicts I), so the violation at k = 2 would never be found.
> - **P2** You know: no path of length 0–50 leads to a violation. You do not know: whether there is a longer one. BMC is bug finding, not a full proof.
> - **P3** Direct (simplest, any solver via a file) → Embedded (no file overhead, API) → Incremental (reuse of learned clauses, assumptions) → Interactive (influence during the search, no need to encode everything up front).
> - **P4** The SAT solver solves the skeleton with CDCL. When the theory finds a contradiction, the negated conflict set (e.g. (¬a ∨ ¬b ∨ ¬c)) is added like a learned clause. It follows from F ∧ T, so it does not change the T-models, but forbids the inconsistent combination forever. A conflict at level 0 means UNSAT.
> - **P5** A formula can have many conflicting subsets of assumptions (tasks 4.2 and 4.3: {¬a, ¬b} and {c, ¬a}). The solver takes the one that comes out of the **very last conflict analysis**, which depends on the order of the assumptions and the course of the search.

> [!warning] Common pitfalls
> - In the DIMACS header `p cnf m n`: m is the **maximum variable index** (not the number of variables), n is the number of clauses. Every clause is terminated by **0**.
> - BMC asks about **exactly** k steps (in the simplified version from the slides). UNSAT for k says nothing about k + 1.
> - Assumptions vanish after every `solve`. Clauses from `add` stay forever. IPASIR has no "delete", hence activation variables.
> - UNSAT **under assumptions** is not UNSAT of the formula.
> - The unsat core is a subset of the **assumptions**, not of the clauses. It need not be unique or minimal.
> - `ipasir_val` only after SAT, `ipasir_failed` only after UNSAT. Every `add` / `assume` discards the previous result.
> - In lazy SMT, a false skeleton literal means the **negated atom** (¬b ↔ x ≠ y + 1).
> - Congruence works in one direction: equal arguments ⇒ equal values, not the other way round.
> - An SMT solver accepts formulas **not in CNF**. CNF is needed only for the skeleton in the SAT solver.
